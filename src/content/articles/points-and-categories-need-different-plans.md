Start with the score your league actually uses. A ranking built for another format can answer a different question from the one your team faces. Your comparison method should match your scoring rules.

This guide offers a framework, not player projections. Check your league’s settings before you apply it. Custom rules can change the value of the same statistical profile.

## In points leagues, read the weights

Write out the assigned point values. Include any penalties and bonuses. Then use one consistent set of assumptions when you compare players. Avoid combining one player’s optimistic estimate with another player’s cautious estimate.

Separate the expected contribution from uncertainty. Consider playing time, role, and availability as questions to research, rather than reasons to invent an exact forecast. If your evidence is thin, make the range wider.

## In category leagues, look at the shape

A category profile shows where a player may help or hurt your team. Keep the categories visible instead of flattening every choice into one total. Ask which strengths your roster already has and which weaknesses are becoming costly.

Compare percentage categories with care. An average of individual percentages does not necessarily describe your team. Attempt volume matters. Use your league’s team totals when you review the actual effect.

## Make tradeoffs deliberate

Some category managers choose to give less weight to a weak category. This approach is often called punting. Treat it as a deliberate tradeoff, not an excuse to ignore every weakness that develops.

Write down what the choice gives you and what it costs. Avoid adding another weak category by accident. In a points format, return to the scoring weights rather than importing a category plan unchanged.

## Use one comparison sheet

Give each candidate the same questions: what is the expected role, what evidence supports it, what fits this roster, and what could change? Keep an uncertainty note beside the answer.

A consistent method makes your decisions easier to explain. It also makes them easier to revise. You can update an assumption without rebuilding your whole draft plan around one surprising performance.
