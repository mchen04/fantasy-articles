A draft rarely follows your preferred order. Build a board that gives you alternatives before the clock starts. Tiers group players you consider reasonably close in value, using the scoring rules you already checked.

This guide does not provide current player tiers. It explains how to build and use your own board from public, current evidence. Keep the assumptions visible.

## Give each tier a reason

A tier should express a meaningful comparison. Group options that offer a similar expected contribution or a similar balance of benefit and uncertainty. Do not divide a list into equal-sized chunks just to make it look organized.

Write a short note at each boundary. It might explain a drop in expected role, a change in roster fit, or a larger evidence gap. If you cannot explain the boundary, reconsider it.

## Track the room without chasing it

Cross off drafted players and count the useful options left in each tier. A run at one position can make another position easier to fill. It can also leave you short of a required slot.

Before reacting, compare the next option with the alternatives you may see later. The fact that someone else makes a pick does not prove you should copy it. Return to your roster and the remaining board.

## Keep a short queue

Prepare a few candidates before your turn. Give each a reason and a condition that would change the choice. This queue helps you make a calm decision when another manager takes your first option.

Include a backup for the roster slot you need most. Keep the queue short enough to review under the clock. A long list with no priorities simply recreates the original ranking problem.

## Review the roster between picks

After each selection, check your active slots, scoring balance, and exposure to uncertainty. Adjust the next queue instead of defending your original plan at every turn.

A tiered board gives you structure while leaving room for judgment. Save your notes after the draft. They show which assumptions you can check when the season gives you real evidence.
