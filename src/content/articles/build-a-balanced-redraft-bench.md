Your bench should serve your active lineup. Start by assigning each spot a job. A collection of interesting names can become hard to manage if none of them solves a clear roster problem.

The right mix depends on bench size, lineup rules, and available players. This guide offers roles to consider. It does not recommend a fixed number of spots for every league.

## Keep usable cover

Check which active positions become difficult to fill when someone is unavailable. Look for cover that fits those slots and your scoring priorities. Eligibility helps only when you can place the player in a lineup you are allowed to change.

Review your league’s lock rules before you count on a substitute. A backup cannot repair a locked lineup after the deadline. Make the decision while the slot is still available to change.

## Define an upside spot

An upside spot tests a possible improvement. Write down the change you hope to see and the public evidence you will use to check it. A clearer role can matter more than one exciting box score.

Set a review point instead of making an endless promise to wait. If the evidence does not develop, compare the player with the best usable replacement. The original draft cost does not add new value today.

## Leave room for short-term work

A flexible spot can support a planned weekly move. Before using it, check the acquisition limit, waiver rules, lineup openings, and cost of the player you would drop.

Do not call every bench spot flexible. Some players protect important roles. Repeated moves can also consume time and acquisitions without improving your active lineup.

## Review the jobs, not just the names

When a roster change fills a gap, a bench role may become redundant. Reassign the spot. Ask whether it now needs to provide cover, test an opportunity, or support the next week.

Keep a brief reason beside each job. This makes a drop decision less personal and more useful. You are choosing the best use of a limited slot, not grading how much you like a player.
